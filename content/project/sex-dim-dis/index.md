  The most striking differences among individuals in a population are often those between the sexes. Such dimorphism is often characterized by obvious differences in morphology and behavior, as well as in a number of differences in physiological functions, including immunity, metabolism, and disease outcome. The sex of the host influences both the dynamics of infections and host symptoms. The sexual dimorphism regarding the response to infection is generally seen as a by-product of the sex-specific roles in reproduction (e.g. males in mammals have a lower immune efficiency because of the physicial interaction between antibodies and testosterone). <b>I am working on the hypothesis that the sexual dimorphism is partly due to the adaptation of the hosts and of the parasites in response to sex-specific exposure to infections.</b>

  In collaboration with the lab of [Jennifer Regan](http://reganlab.bio.ed.ac.uk/), we have reviewed that <i>Drosophila</i> are almost always dimorphic when it comes to respond to an infection [(Belmonte et al., 2020)](Belmonte_Front.Immuno_2020.pdf). 
  {{< figure src="SexDim_droso.jpg" title="Schematic representing sexual dimorphism in <i>Drosophila</i> innate immunity at basal state." >}}
   
  
  During my time in Cornell, in the Laboratory of [Brian Lazzaro](http://www.lazzaro.entomology.cornell.edu/index.html), we have shown that part of the reason for this dimorphism is due to a difference in resistance. Using statistical methods, we were able to compare the dynamic intra-host between sexes and we have shown that males control the bacterial proliferation on average few hours earlier than females which makes for the whole difference in survival [(Duneau et al., 2017)](Duneau_BMCBiology_2017.pdf). 
  {{< figure src="SexDim_WHD.jpg" width="70%" title="Within-host dynamics of <i>P. rettgeri</i>. Males controlled proliferation on average faster than females. (Drawings by @joana_gcc)" >}}  
  
  Using whole genome transcriptomics and functional genetic tools, that allow us to modify host characteristics, we showed that this difference in resistance was due to a difference in innate immunity. 
  
  {{< figure src="SexDim_RNAseq.jpg" width="40%" title="Transcriptomic analysis comparing the response to infection (8h) in males and females. The induction of the AMP Drosomycin, from the Toll pathway, was largely sexually dimorphic." >}}  
  
  More particularly, males and females differ in their recognition of Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacterial infection by the Toll pathway via the Persephone branch of the immune system. Hence, our results suggest not only that studies on immunity should include both sexes, a practice that concern so far only 14% of the studies with (<i>Drosophila</i>) [(Belmonte et al., 2020)](Belmonte_Front.Immuno_2020.pdf), but also that both sexes represents different environments to their parasites, an observation that is likely to play a role on parasite evolution.

  The infections above were systemic: the bacteria were injected directly into the body of the fly. Yet many pathogens enter their host through the gut. With the lab of [Igor Iatsenko](https://www.zibi-berlin.de/research/faculty/principal-investigators/iatsenko/index.html) at the Max Planck Institute for Infection Biology in Berlin, and Marko Rubinić, the PhD student we co-supervised, we studied the intestinal infection of <i>Drosophila</i> by <i>Pseudomonas entomophila</i>, a bacterium originally isolated from fruit flies that kills insects but is harmless to humans [(Rubinić et al., 2026)](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2514992123). The dimorphism was striking: after feeding on the bacterium, most males survived, while 70 to 80% of the females died. This was not a peculiarity of a few laboratory strains. Across 183 genetically distinct lines of the <i>Drosophila</i> Genetic Reference Panel, 105 of the 120 lines with a significant sex difference were more susceptible in females. The dimorphism persisted whether males and females were kept together or apart, across doses and temperatures, in virgin females and in germ-free flies, and both sexes ingested the same amount of bacteria. Surprisingly, the classical defenses of the gut did not explain it: flies lacking the Imd pathway, the Toll pathway or an intact peritrophic matrix were still dimorphic.

  The difference lay instead in the ability to flush the pathogen out. Males started to clear the bacteria within 2 hours and had cleared them by about 6 hours, whereas females kept high loads. The infection almost completely blocked defecation in females, much less so in males, whose guts kept contracting. When we impaired gut contractions in males (mutants for TRPA1 or DH31), they became as susceptible as females; using a GWAS on the genetic lines, we also identified a potassium transporter, CG4928, whose knockdown in the gut almost abolished male defecation and reduced the sex difference in survival. Conversely, stimulating gut contractions in females brought their survival close to that of males. Why does the female gut stop? The infection triggers a strong oxidative stress in the female gut, but not in the male gut. Males have a higher basal carbohydrate metabolism, with a more active pentose phosphate pathway and more NADPH, a key fuel of antioxidant defenses. Males lacking two enzymes of this pathway accumulated reactive oxygen species, stopped defecating and died like females, while boosting the antioxidant enzyme SOD1 in the female gut restored defecation and improved survival.

  Finally, and this is for me the most exciting part, we also looked at the pathogen. By measuring bacterial proteins directly within the guts of infected flies, we found that <i>P. entomophila</i> does not behave the same way in males and in females. Several bacterial proteins were more abundant in the female gut, the most different being Hfq, an RNA chaperone known to regulate virulence and stress resistance in bacteria. A mutant lacking Hfq could no longer kill flies of either sex and was more sensitive to oxidants and to an antimicrobial compound. Hence, the metabolic environment of the gut does not only shape the defenses of the host, it also changes how the pathogen expresses its virulence. This gives direct support to the idea that males and females are different environments for a parasite [(Duneau and Ebert, 2012)](Duneau_PLoSBiology_2012.pdf): the pathogen responds to the sex of its host, and such sex-specific expression of virulence is the raw material on which selection can act to produce parasites adapted to one host sex (see below).

  Sex differences in the response to infection also change over the life of an individual. It is widely accepted that susceptibility to infection increases with age, and this is usually attributed to a decline of the immune system (i.e. immunosenescence). Again with the lab of [Jennifer Regan](http://reganlab.bio.ed.ac.uk/), we tested this assumption in both sexes, using an outbred population and 22 genetically distinct lines of <i>Drosophila melanogaster</i> infected with several bacterial pathogens [(Monteiro-Black, Corbally et al., 2026)](Monteiro-Black_BMCBiology_2026.pdf). Susceptibility increased early in life in both sexes, with each doubling of age approximately doubling susceptibility. Susceptibility of males and females was correlated in young flies, but no longer in old flies. Using the pathogen load upon death, a measure that allows us to disentangle resistance from tolerance without targeting a specific mechanism [(Duneau et al., 2025)](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.104052), we showed that the age-related increase in susceptibility was sometimes due to a genuine decline in resistance, but more often to a decline in the ability to tolerate the infection, which could already be detected early in life. Finally, susceptibility at old age predicted lifespan in males only. We therefore argue that the term "immunosenescence" should be restricted to the decline of resistance mechanisms, and that population variation, sex, and the ageing of non-immune functions must be considered to understand why old individuals lose control of their infections.

 I have been interested in parasite sex-specific evolution since when I was working with <i>Daphnia</i>, a small crustacean living in ponds throughout the world. Research on the <i>Daphnia</i> model has almost always been done in females. This is due to the fact that in most natural conditions the populations are overwhelmingly female. Such extreme bias in sex-ratio, provided the perfect setting to test whether parasites in those populations were better adapted to the characteristics of the sex they encountered the most frequenty. Together with my PhD supervisor [Dieter Ebert](http://www.evolution.unibas.ch/ebert/), we first proposed that a parasite is all the more likely to adapt to one sex that, on the one hand, it proliferates more often in one sex than in the other and, on the other hand, the differences between the sexes are important [(Duneau and Ebert, 2012)](Duneau_PLoSBiology_2012.pdf). In this article, I list a number of conditions under which parasites can be expected to adapt to one sex of their host. I argue on the basis of examples that the sex differences in symptoms and prevalence described for many parasites are partly explained by the properties of the parasite and not only, as is commonly claimed, by those of the host.

{{< figure src="Figure_sex_dim.jpg" title="Sex specific adaptation of parasites. More a parasite evolves within one host sex and greater is the host sexual dimorphism, more likely the parasite will adapt specifically to the characteristic of its host" >}}

We then tested my hypothesis with <i>Daphnia magna</i> and its parasitic bacterium <i>Pasteuria ramosa</i> [Duneau et al., 2012](Duneau_PLoSBiology_2012.pdf). This pathogen evolves in host populations that are predominantly female and we were able to show that, as expected, it exploited females better than males. The parasite was known to induce castration of its host (female) as an adaptation to induce gigantism and increase the carrying capacity of its environment. We confirmed that the parasite induced an increase in carrying capacity through gigantism in females. However, if the males were also castrated by the infection, this had no effect on their size. In fact, the adaptation that is advantageous when the females are infected (the common host) is not advantageous when the males are infected. We therefore revealed a sex-specific adaptation of a parasite in nature.
